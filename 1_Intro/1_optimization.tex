\section{Stochastic Optimization}
\label{sec:ch1:intro}

Machine learning has seen a rapid development in recent years. For example, the recent outbreak of large language models (LLMs) has the potential to have significant impact to many areas, including medical diagnosis and clinical decision support \citep{clusmann2023future,thirunavukarasu2023large}, accelerating scientific discovery \citep{lin2022evolutionary}, enhancing developer productivity in software engineering \citep{peng2023impact}, improving quality and accessibility of education \citep{kasneci2023chatgpt}.

At the core of its success, machine learning consists of three important components: data collection, model design, and optimization. Data collection is the process of gathering data from various sources, model design is the process of designing a model to solve a specific problem, and optimization is the process of training the model to minimize a loss function. While all three components are equally important, this thesis focuses on the last component, optimizing machine learning models.

Formally, the optimization problem can be generally formulated as follows: given an objective function $f: \RR^d \to \RR$, the problem is to solve
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\vecx\in\RR^d} f(\vecx),
    \label{eqn:ch1:optimization}
\end{equation}
where $\vecx$ denotes the parameters of the model and $\xi$ denotes data randomly sampled from an unknown distribution. Specifically, the problem where the objective $f$ is explicitly known is called the \emph{deterministic optimization problem}. Under this setting, algorithms are assumed to have access to the exact objective function $f(\vecx)$ and/or its gradient $\nabla f(\vecx)$. While there has been extensive research on this problem \cite{nemirovskii1979complexity,nesterov1983method,boyd2004convex}, it is not the focus of this thesis because of its discrepancy between the pratical setting of training modern machine learning models. 

In most of the practical settings, training models often requires minimizing a loss that depends on an unknown data distribution. This problem is known as the \emph{stochastic optimization problem} and can be formally defined as follows:
\begin{equation}
    \min_{\vecx\in\RR^d} f(\vecx) = \E_{\xi}[F(\vecx,\xi)],
    \label{eqn:ch1:stochastic-optimization}
\end{equation}
where $F$ denotes a stochastic estimator of the objective function $f$ and $\xi$ denotes some data randomly sampled from an unknown distribution. For example, suppose we want to train a vision model to classify images into different categories. Then $\vecx$ could be the parameters of the model, the data $\xi$ could be a tuple of an image and its ground truth label, and $F$ could be the cross-entropy loss between the model prediction and its corresponding label. 

To solve this problem, an algorithm is given access to a (stochastic) \emph{oracle} that can evaluate the stochastic estimator $F$. In particular, given a pair $(\vecx,\xi)$, a \emph{first-order oracle} returns the (sub)gradient $\nabla F(\vecx,\xi)$ which is evaluated by performing reverse mode auto-differentiation (commonly known as backprop). In less common settings where backprop is prohibitively expensive or even impossible, a \emph{zeroth-order oracle} that returns the function value $F(\vecx,\xi)$ becomes a good substitute. 

Since it's only possible to query a finite amount of floats within finite amount of time, it is impractical to find an exact solution of stochastic optimization defined in \eqref{eqn:ch1:stochastic-optimization}. For example, consider the objective $f(x)=|x-x^\star|$ for some randomly chosen and unknown $x^\star$. Even without stochasticity, there is no known algorithm that could find the exact minimizer $\vecx^\star$. What is possible though is finding a solution that is close to $0$. Consequently, the goal of stochastic optimization is to find an \emph{approximate} solution that is sufficiently good.

The definition of such an approximate solution essentially depends on the nature of the objective. Indeed, it is intuitive that we could only hope for a less optimal solution if the objective is more difficult. Such difficulty is characterized by the convexity and smoothness of the objective whose formal definition is deferred to Section \ref{sec:ch1:prelim}. When the objective is convex, the goal is to find an $\epsilon$-suboptimal point $\vecx$ such that 
\begin{equation}
    f(\vecx) - \inf f < \epsilon.
    \label{eqn:ch1:eps-suboptimal}
\end{equation}
When the objective is non-convex, even finding a suboptimal point is impossible. Instead, the goal is to find an $\epsilon$-stationary point $\vecx$ such that 
\begin{equation}
    \| \nabla f(\vecx) \| < \epsilon.
    \label{eqn:ch1:eps-stationary}
\end{equation}
In either setting, the performance of an optimization algorithm is often measured by its \emph{oracle complexity}, that is the number of oracle queries it requires in order to find an $\epsilon$-suboptimal ($\epsilon$-stationary) point. The fewer oracle queries it requires, the more efficient the algorithm is.


\subsection{History of Development}

As aforementioned, stochastic optimization algorithms aim to find an approximate solution of \eqref{eqn:ch1:stochastic-optimization} using stochastic oracles. The most classical and widely used algorithm is stochastic gradient descent (SGD), which updates the parameter iteratively according to
\begin{equation}
    \vecx_{t+1} = \vecx_t - \eta_t \, \vecg_t, \qquad \vecg_t = \nabla F(\vecx_t, \xi_t),
    \label{eqn:ch1:sgd-update}
\end{equation}
where $\eta_t$ is the learning rate (or step size) and $\vecg_t$ is a stochastic gradient at $\vecx_t$ obtained from a random sample $\xi_t$. Remarkably, SGD and its variants have been the most popular optimizers for training machine learning models, due to their simple implementation and good empirical performance.

The theoretical performance of SGD has been extensively studied and is well-known by far. When the objective is convex, SGD finds an $\epsilon$-suboptimal point defined in \eqref{eqn:ch1:eps-suboptimal} with $T=O(\epsilon^2)$ oracle complexity \citep{zinkevich2003online}. In non-convex but smooth setting, \cite{ghadimi2013stochastic} proved that SGD finds an $\epsilon$-stationary point defined in \eqref{eqn:ch1:eps-stationary} with $T=O(\epsilon^4)$ complexity. Notably, both rates are proven to be the lower bound in their corresponding setting \citep{nesterov2013introductory,bubeck2015convex,arjevani2022lower}, suggesting that SGD achieves the tight oracle complexity in theory although being a very simple algorithm. 

Despite the fact that SGD has achieved the optimal complexity, a rich family of algorithms beyond vanilla SGD has been continuously developed, and they have shown great empirical performances. Among all, one line of algorithm focuses on the \emph{momentum methods} \citep{polyak1964speeding,sutskever2013importance}. Instead of using the instantaneous gradient as the update, momentum methods aggregate a running average of past gradients to stabilize updates\footnote{There are different ways to formulate the momentum update. Here we choose the notion that is most common in later algorithms.}:
\begin{equation}
    \vecx_{t+1} = \vecx_t - \eta_t\, \vecm_t,
    \qquad \vecm_t = (1-\beta)\, \vecm_{t-1} + \beta\, \vecg_t.
    \label{eqn:ch1:momentum-update}
\end{equation}
Here $\beta$ is the momentum constant: as $\beta$ becomes closer to $0$, the momentum puts more weight on past gradients. \cite{sutskever2013importance} observed that SGD with momentum (SGDM) outperforms vanilla SGD when training various models, especially by accelerating the training through smooth loss landscapes. Later, various works have proposed many variants of SGDM, including but not limited to Adam \citep{kingma2014adam}, Lars \citep{you2017scaling}, Lamb \citep{you2019large}, LaProp \citep{ziyin2020laprop}, Lion \citep{chen2023symbolic}, etc. All of them have shown better performance than vanilla SGD and SGDM.

Besides momentum, another line of research studies algorithm adaptivity, which started with the AdaGrad algorithm developed independently by \citep{duchi10adagrad,mcmahan2010adaptive}. In particular, AdaGrad adjusts the learning rate on-the-fly:
\begin{equation}
    \vecx_{t+1} = \vecx_t - \eta_t\, \vecg_t,
    \qquad \eta_t = \frac{c}{\sqrt{\epsilon + \sum_{i=1}^t \vecg_t^2}}.
    \label{eqn:ch1:adagrad-update}
\end{equation}
where the operation in $\eta_t$ is computed coordinate-wise. Such adaptive method effectively improves the performance when the objective is ill-conditioned. For example, when the stochastic gradients are sparse, AdaGrad could effectively optimize all coordinates by decreasing the learning rates in the frequently observed coordinates while keeping that of the sparse coordinates relatively larger. 

AdaGrad has also inspired many new algorithms. Notably, Adam \citep{kingma2014adam} combines the intuition of momentum and AdaGrad, and it has become one of the most popular optimizer in both literature and industry. Other subsequent works focus on designing more efficient preconditioning method, such as Shampoo \citep{gupta2018shampoo}, signed SGD \citep{bernstein2018signsgd}, AdaBelief \citep{zhuang2020adabelief}, SOAP \citep{vyas2025soap}, Muon \citep{jordan2024muon}, etc.

Another key development in stochastic optimization is the advent of variance-reduction techniques, which aim to improve the efficiency of stochastic gradient methods by reducing the noise inherent in gradient estimates. These algorithms include SAG \citep{schmidt2017minimizing}, SVRG \citep{johnson2013accelerating}, SAGA \citep{defazio2014saga}, Natasha \citep{allenzhu2018natasha}, Spider \citep{fang2018spider}, Storm \citep{cutkosky2019momentum}, etc. At its core, variance-reduction algorithms use the sum of gradient difference estimators to estimate the gradient:
\begin{equation}
    \vecg_t = \sum_{i=1}^t \vecd_i, \qquad \vecd_i = \nabla F(\vecx_i,\xi_i) - \nabla F(\vecx_{i-1}, \xi_i).
    \label{eqn:ch1:variance-reduction}
\end{equation}
Under a slightly stronger smoothness assumption called \emph{mean-squared smoothness}, this estimator has reduced variance, allowing the algorithms to achieve $O(\epsilon^3)$ complexity, improving from the $O(\epsilon^4)$ under standard first-order smoothness assumption. On the other hand, although variance-reduction algorithms have better theoretical convergence rate, they are not favored in pratice due to its additional computation cost---two gradient computations in each iteration are required in order to compute the gradient difference. It is also observed that SVRG does not perform well in pratical trainining tasks \citep{defazio2019ineffectiveness}.
